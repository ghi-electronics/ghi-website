# Distance Sensor

Bats do it, ships do it, and so does the sensor in the kit: send out a sound pulse, wait for the echo, and work out the distance from how long it took. The HC-SR04 does the ultrasound part; your code does the timing and the arithmetic.

This is also the lesson where a part needs **5 V**, which is a good moment to learn what "5 V tolerant" means.

About 35 minutes.

## What you'll learn

- How an ultrasonic sensor measures distance by timing an echo
- How to measure a pulse in microseconds without writing timing code yourself
- How to convert time into distance using the speed of sound
- What 5 V tolerance means — and what it does not mean

## What you'll need

- From the kit:
  - HC-SR04 ultrasonic distance sensor
  - Piezo buzzer
  - Jumper wires

## Step 3 — Measure the echo

The sensor works in two steps: you pulse **Trig** high for at least 10 µs, and it replies by holding **Echo** high for exactly as long as the sound takes to travel out and back.

Ten microseconds is far too short to produce with `Thread.Sleep`, and timing the echo in a loop would be hopeless. `PulseFeedback` does both in hardware-assisted form: one call sends the trigger and returns how long the echo lasted.

<Tabs groupId="board">
<TabItem value="flea" label="FEZ Flea" default>

```csharp title="Program.cs"
using System;
using System.Diagnostics;
using System.Threading;
using GHIElectronics.TinyCLR.Devices.Gpio;
using GHIElectronics.TinyCLR.Devices.Signals;
using GHIElectronics.TinyCLR.Pins;

var gpio = GpioController.GetDefault();

// highlight-start
var trigger = gpio.OpenPin(FEZFlea.GpioPin.PB14);
var echo = gpio.OpenPin(FEZFlea.GpioPin.PB15);
// highlight-end

var sensor = new PulseFeedback(trigger, echo, PulseFeedbackMode.EchoDuration) {
    DisableInterrupts = false,
    Timeout = TimeSpan.FromSeconds(1),
    PulseLength = TimeSpan.FromTicks(100),      // 10 µs trigger pulse
    PulseValue = GpioPinValue.High,             // trigger is an active-high pulse
    EchoValue = GpioPinValue.High,              // measure how long echo stays high
};

while (true) {
    TimeSpan echoTime = sensor.Trigger();

    double microseconds = echoTime.TotalMilliseconds * 1000;
    double centimeters = microseconds * 0.0343 / 2;

    Debug.WriteLine("Echo: " + microseconds.ToString("N0") + " µs   " +
                    "Distance: " + centimeters.ToString("N1") + " cm");

    Thread.Sleep(200);
}
```

Press **F5** and hold your hand in front of the sensor. Move it closer and further, and check the numbers against a ruler.

- **`PulseLength`** is the trigger pulse. One tick is 100 ns, so 100 ticks is 10 µs.
- **`Trigger()`** does the whole exchange and hands back the echo duration.
- **`Timeout`** caps how long it waits. Nothing to bounce off means no echo — aim it at an open room and the readings stop making sense.

**Try the debugger:** set a breakpoint on `Debug.WriteLine(...)`, hold your hand at a fixed distance, and press **F5** a few times. `microseconds` and `centimeters` in the Locals window show one complete measurement each time — and you'll see a little variation between identical-looking readings.

## Step 4 — Where the numbers come from

Sound travels about **343 metres per second** in room-temperature air, which is **0.0343 cm per microsecond**. The echo made the trip **twice** — out to the object and back — so half the time is the distance:

```
centimeters = microseconds × 0.0343 ÷ 2
```

A few numbers worth knowing:

| Distance | Echo duration |
|---|---|
| 5 cm | ~290 µs |
| 50 cm | ~2,900 µs |
| 2 m | ~11,700 µs |

For inches, divide the centimeters by 2.54 — or use 0.0135 inches per microsecond in the formula.

## Step 5 — Build a parking sensor

Readings become useful when something reacts to them. This version beeps faster the closer an object gets, like the sensor on a car bumper. Add to the top of your program:

```csharp
using PwmChannel buzzer = PwmChannel.Create(
    FEZFlea.Timer.Pwm.Controller16.Chip,
    FEZFlea.Timer.Pwm.Controller16.PB8,
    2000,
    0);

buzzer.Start();
```

Then replace the `while` loop with:

```csharp
while (true) {
    double microseconds = sensor.Trigger().TotalMilliseconds * 1000;
    double centimeters = microseconds * 0.0343 / 2;

    Debug.WriteLine("Distance: " + centimeters.ToString("N1") + " cm");

    if (centimeters < 5) {
        // Very close: one continuous tone.
        buzzer.DutyCycle = 0.5;
        Thread.Sleep(200);
    } else if (centimeters < 60) {
        // Closer means a shorter gap between beeps.
        buzzer.DutyCycle = 0.5;
        Thread.Sleep(30);

        buzzer.DutyCycle = 0;
        Thread.Sleep((int)(centimeters * 8));
    } else {
        // Nothing nearby: stay quiet.
        buzzer.DutyCycle = 0;
        Thread.Sleep(200);
    }
}
```

Press **F5** and walk your hand toward the sensor. The beeps speed up as you approach, then run together into one tone.

## Step 6 — A radar is a distance sensor that moves

Radar displays look impressive, but the idea is simple: a distance sensor that **points somewhere different each time it measures**. Mount the HC-SR04 on the servo from [Servo Motor](servo-motor.mdx), and at every angle in a sweep, take a reading. Angle plus distance describes a point — and a series of them describes the room.

Watch for two practical details. Give the servo a moment to settle before measuring, since a moving sensor hears its own echo from the wrong direction. And the beam is wide, so a sweep sees a smeared shape rather than a crisp outline.

## How it works

### Ultrasound

The sensor's two cylinders are a speaker and a microphone, working at **40 kHz** — well above human hearing, so all of this is silent to you. A trigger pulse makes the speaker emit a short burst of clicks, and the chip holds **Echo** high until the microphone hears them come back.

```
Trig   ▁▇▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁   10 µs pulse from your board
Echo   ▁▁▁▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▇▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁▁   sensor holds this high for the round trip
```

The practical range is roughly **2 cm to 4 m**. Closer than 2 cm, the echo returns before the sensor is listening. Further than 4 m, too little sound comes back.

What it's good at: flat, hard surfaces facing it. What it struggles with: soft fabric and foam, which absorb the sound; surfaces at an angle, which bounce it away; and small objects, which the wide beam (roughly 15°) tends to miss between larger ones.

### Temperature matters

The speed of sound depends on air temperature — about 0.6 m/s faster per degree Celsius. From a cold morning to a hot afternoon, that's about a 2% shift, which is 2 cm over a metre. Good enough for a parking sensor, and something to correct for if you ever need better than a few percent.

### 5 V tolerance

Your board runs at 3.3 V: its pins drive 3.3 V when high, and expect no more than that on an input. **5 V tolerant** means the input pins can accept a 5 V signal anyway, without damage — which is why the sensor's 5 V **Echo** output connects straight to a pin, with no divider.

Two things it does **not** mean:

- **You can't power the board from 5 V.** Tolerance applies to input pins only.
- **Your board can't send 5 V.** Output pins still drive 3.3 V. That's fine here: the HC-SR04 reads anything above about 2 V as high, so a 3.3 V trigger works. Some 5 V parts don't, and they need a level shifter.

[GPIO](../feature/gpio.mdx#digital-inputs) has the full details.

## Try this

1. **Tape measure.** Average ten readings and print the result to one decimal place. Compare it with a ruler at 10 cm, 50 cm and 1 m, and see where it drifts.
2. **Steady the edge.** A threshold at "closer than 30 cm" will chatter when something sits right at 30 cm. Apply the hysteresis from [Light and Logic](light-sensor.mdx#step-6--hysteresis).
3. **Invisible tripwire.** Watch for a sudden drop in distance and sound an alarm — a doorway counter, or a drawer alarm.
4. **Theremin.** Map distance onto the buzzer's pitch from [Sound](sound.mdx) and play it by waving your hand.
5. **Radar sweep.** Build Step 6 for real: sweep the servo and print an angle-and-distance table.
